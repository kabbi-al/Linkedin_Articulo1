Curiosity became my competitive edge

Notes on how artificial intelligence and a bit of stubbornness helped me solve problems I didn't know I could solve a year ago: from automating processes to coding the page you're reading.

I've never defined myself by what I already know how to do, but by how fast I can learn what I don't know yet. That's been, without planning it, the common thread of the last few months.

I work at a moment when tools change faster than the manuals do. Generative AI stopped being a curiosity and became a daily work companion: for research, for automation, for coding. Whoever waits for the perfect course or the ideal moment to learn simply falls behind. I chose to learn by doing, mistakes included.

01 Automating candidate research

One of the most valuable parts of any hiring process (and also one of the slowest) is the upfront research: understanding a candidate's background, cross-referencing information, spotting relevant signals before the first conversation. Doing it well by hand can take hours per person.

I set out to use AI to compress that time without losing judgment. I designed a flow that combines search, synthesis, and structuring of information with AI assistants, so what used to take hours of scattered research now arrives organized, summarized, and ready for decision-making in minutes.

What mattered wasn't the tool itself, but learning to design the process: what to ask the AI, how to verify what it returns, and where human judgment is still irreplaceable.

The result wasn't just time saved. It was more time for what truly requires a person: the conversation, the culture-fit assessment, the intuition no model replaces yet.

02 I built this very page myself

The second area where I decided to learn fast was web development. I don't come from a technical programming background, but I understood that being able to build something with your own hands, even a simple site, completely changes the way you communicate with the people who do it every day.

With code assistants like Claude and Copilot as guides, I went from reading HTML without understanding it to laying out full pages: semantic structure, responsive design, a bit of animation, good contrast, visual hierarchy. The page you're reading right now is proof of that.

04 The real skill: learning how to learn

Neither automating research nor building a website was something I knew how to do a little while ago. What I did have was the willingness to sit down, try, break things, and try again with AI as an accelerator, not a shortcut.

I think that's the skill that truly matters today: not stacking certifications or memorizing tools that will be different in a year, but building the ability to adapt quickly when the ground shifts. AI didn't come to replace that curiosity, it came to put it on steroids.

I'm still learning right now, as I write this, and that's honestly the part I enjoy the most.
